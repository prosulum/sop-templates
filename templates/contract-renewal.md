# Contract Renewal

Updated September 28, 2026

This SOP is the cycle an account lead runs for one contract, from the day it enters the renewal window to a signed agreement, a documented decline, or a documented no response, so no renewal lapses without a logged attempt to reach the client. The account lead reviews account health, builds a value recap, sends the proposal, follows up on a fixed calendar, handles price pushback with scope, executes the renewal, and records the outcome. It applies to any service business managing recurring contracts, retainers, or subscriptions, and it starts each time a contract appears on the monthly renewal report.

**What this gives you:** A renewal that opens with what your work changed for the client gives you a record of the difference you make. You keep serving the clients you want to keep, without renegotiating your worth every cycle.

**Primary owner:** Account lead  
**Runs:** Once per contract, starting when the contract first appears on the monthly renewal report, which is at least [renewal window, e.g. 90 days] before the end date  
**Time:** [3 to 5] hours of active work per renewal, spread across the renewal window (example, replace with your own)

## Before you start

- [Your CRM] or [your contract management tool] with contract end dates, values, and terms tracked, and one named account owner per contract
- A monthly renewal report, run as its own procedure by [role] on [day of month]: every contract ending in the next [report window, e.g. 120 days], sorted by value and risk. Keep the report window at least as long as the renewal window plus the time between reports. This SOP starts when a contract appears on it. Write that report as [your monthly renewal report procedure]; it is not part of the steps below
- Your renewal calendar, written as days before the contract end date: proposal at [proposal day, e.g. 60], follow-up email at [email day, e.g. 45], phone call at [call day, e.g. 30], manager escalation at [escalation day, e.g. 15]
- A renewal proposal template, plus the current rate card or scope options if pricing may change
- Account health data: the client health score from [your health score source], support ticket history, engagement or usage data, and payment history
- [Your e-signature tool] with the current contract loaded
- Written discount approval rules: the [approver role, e.g. sales director], the discount the account lead may offer alone ([limit, e.g. none]), and the response time the approver commits to ([N] business days)
- A written rule for a contract that reaches its end date with no answer: [your no-response policy, e.g. a written month-to-month extension for a set number of days, or a lapse notice]

## Procedure

1. **Open the renewal record and review account health** (Owner: Account lead)

   - **a.** Open the contract in [your CRM] and confirm the end date, value, and current terms match the signed agreement, and that you are the named account owner. If you are not, tell [manager role] and stop until an owner is named.
   - **b.** Check the client health score from [your health score source], support ticket history, invoice and payment history, and any notes from the account team.
   - **c.** Confirm who approves the spend and who the day-to-day contact is today. If either changed, record the new contact in the CRM, ask the previous contact for an introduction, and tell [role, e.g. the account lead's manager] before you send anything.
   - **d.** Note any at-risk signal: a usage drop beyond [your usage-drop threshold], an unresolved ticket, a payment more than [N] days late, or a competitor mention.
   - **e.** If you noted a usage drop or a competitor mention, write in the CRM what the value recap will address, and tell [manager role] before you go to step 2.
   - **f.** If there is an unresolved ticket or a late payment, resolve it or agree on a resolution plan with [support lead role], record it in the CRM, and only then go to step 2. See Customer Support Escalation if the ticket is an open escalation.

   *Why this matters:* Renewing a healthy client and rescuing an at-risk one require different plans; skipping this step means you do not know which one you have.

2. **Build the value recap before presenting terms** (Owner: Account lead)

   - **a.** Pull the specific outcomes, deliverables, and metrics from the current term: what was delivered and what measurably changed for the client.
   - **b.** Translate the recap into the client's language: what problem stopped being a problem, not a list of internal activities.
   - **c.** Keep it to one page or a short section of the proposal; a wall of detail buries the message.
   - **d.** If you cannot find a measurable outcome, ask the client's day-to-day contact what changed for them, and write that down in their words.

   *Why this matters:* A renewal that opens with terms and price reads as a bill. A renewal that opens with value delivered reads as a case for continuing.

3. **Send the renewal proposal, value first** (Owner: Account lead)

   - **a.** Build the proposal from [your renewal proposal template]: lead with the value recap, then the renewal term, any scope or pricing changes, and a clear call to action.
   - **b.** State a response date and what happens if the client does not respond by then: [your no-response policy].
   - **c.** Check the terms against the signed agreement and [your rate card] before sending. If the price or scope changes, have [reviewer role] check it too, and record each check with initials and the date in the CRM record.
   - **d.** Send the proposal at [proposal day, e.g. 60] days before the contract end date, using the wording below. Copy the support lead if the account had an escalation in the last [N] days, so the team is aligned before the client responds.
   - **e.** Log the send date in the CRM. Step 4 uses it in the follow-up wording. The follow-up days count back from the contract end date.

   > **Use this wording: Proposal cover message**
   > 
   > Hi [client contact name], as we approach the end of your current term on [contract end date], I wanted to share what we accomplished together: [one-line value recap]. The attached proposal covers the renewal: [term], [scope or pricing changes, or "no changes"]. Could you let me know by [response date] whether you would like to move ahead, or if you would like to talk it through first? I can make time on [two proposed times].

   *Why this matters:* Proposals that arrive without the value context in front of them get treated as pure negotiation from the first line.

4. **Track the response and follow up on schedule** (Owner: Account lead)

   - **a.** If the client accepts, go to step 6.
   - **b.** If the client pushes back on price or scope, go to step 5.
   - **c.** If there is no acknowledgment by [email day, e.g. 45] days before the end date, send the follow-up email below.
   - **d.** If there is still no response by [call day, e.g. 30] days before the end date, call the client and use the call wording below. Email alone is not enough this close to expiration.
   - **e.** If there is still no response by [escalation day, e.g. 15] days before the end date, tell [manager role] and treat the account as at-risk regardless of the prior health score.
   - **f.** If there is still no response on the contract end date, apply [your no-response policy], tell [manager role] what you applied, and go to step 7 with the outcome "no response".

   > **Use this wording: Follow-up email**
   > 
   > Hi [client contact name], following up on the renewal proposal I sent on [proposal send date]. Do you have any questions I can answer before [contract end date]? If it helps, I can walk through it on a short call.

   > **Use this wording: Phone call opening**
   > 
   > Hi [client contact name], this is [your name] from [your company]. I sent the renewal proposal on [proposal send date] and wanted to make sure it reached you. Is there anything in it you would like to change or talk through before [contract end date]?

   *Why this matters:* A contract lapsing because no one followed up is a process failure, not a client decision, and a documented calendar is what makes sure someone follows up.

5. **Handle price pushback with scope, not a discount** (Owner: Account lead)

   - **a.** Ask the client what specifically feels out of line with the value delivered before offering any concession.
   - **b.** Respond with a scope conversation first: a shorter term, a smaller deliverable set, or a paid add-on for the same rate, rather than cutting price for the same scope. Use the wording below.
   - **c.** If the client agrees to a scope option, send the revised proposal after the check in step 3 and go to step 6 when the client accepts.
   - **d.** If the client turns the scope options down and you have no documented reason for a discount, go to step 7 with the outcome "declined". If you do have one, follow the discount actions below.
   - **e.** If the client goes quiet, go back to step 4.
   - **f.** Discount only for a documented reason (a real service gap, a multi-year commitment, a deliberate strategic account decision). Any discount above [limit, e.g. none] needs approval from [approver role] before you offer it. Send the request with the reason, the amount, and the contract value.
   - **g.** If the approver approves, record the approval and the reason in the CRM, send the revised proposal after the check in step 3, and go to step 6 when the client accepts. If the client goes quiet, go back to step 4.
   - **h.** If the approver denies the discount, offer the scope options only. If the client still will not renew on the approved terms, go to step 7 with the outcome "declined".
   - **i.** If the approver has not answered in [N] business days, ask [backup approver role] and do not offer the discount until one of them approves.

   > **Use this wording: Price pushback reply**
   > 
   > Thanks for telling me that, [client contact name]. Before we talk about price, what part of the proposal feels out of line with what you have gotten from us? Depending on that, we can look at a [shorter term], a [smaller set of deliverables], or [an add-on] at the current rate.

   *Why this matters:* Discounting on renewal without a scope change can teach clients to ask for a discount every cycle and can cut margin on accounts you already won.

6. **Execute the renewal and update every system of record** (Owner: Account lead)

   - **a.** Send the final agreement for signature with [your e-signature tool] and confirm it shows as completed. Save the countersigned copy in [contract folder location]. That copy is the artifact for this step.
   - **b.** Update the contract end date, value, and terms in the CRM the same day it is signed.
   - **c.** If the price or scope changed, send the new terms to [billing role] so the next invoice reflects them. Billing follows the Client Invoicing procedure.
   - **d.** Ask [billing role] to reply confirming the new terms are in [your billing tool], and attach that reply to the CRM record. If there is no reply in [N] business days, tell [manager role].
   - **e.** Tell support and delivery that the renewal is closed and the account continues under the new terms.

   *Why this matters:* A signed renewal that is not reflected everywhere creates billing errors and confused handoffs a few weeks later.

7. **Document the outcome, win or lose** (Owner: Account lead)

   - **a.** If the client declines, capture the stated reason in the CRM in their own words, and note whether it was price, scope, service, or a business change on their end.
   - **b.** If the client renews, log the new term length, value, and any scope change so the next renewal cycle starts with accurate history.
   - **c.** If the outcome is "no response", log the dates of each attempt and which no-response policy you applied.
   - **d.** Close the renewal task in the CRM. The cycle for this contract ends here; the next one starts when the contract reappears on the monthly renewal report.

   *Why this matters:* Decline reasons show why clients leave, but only if they get captured instead of filed away.

## Exceptions and troubleshooting

- **If** The client has gone quiet after the proposal and is past the call day. **Then:** Call, tell [manager role], and treat the account as at-risk regardless of prior health score. If the end date arrives with no answer, apply your no-response policy.
- **If** The client wants to renew but at a lower price with no change in scope. **Then:** Hold the line with a scope trade (shorter term, fewer deliverables, or a paid add-on) rather than a straight discount. Ask [approver role] if the client will not move off price alone.
- **If** Legal or procurement on the client side is holding up signature past the end date. **Then:** Put a short written bridge agreement or month-to-month extension in place, if your written rule allows it, so service does not lapse while paperwork finishes. If your no-response policy is a lapse notice, do not send it to a client who is still working toward signature; use a written extension instead. Do not let the account run unpaid while waiting.
- **If** The account has real unresolved issues heading into the renewal window. **Then:** Address and document the resolution before sending the proposal (step 1). A renewal proposal sent over an open issue reads as tone-deaf and can raise churn risk.
- **If** You want to see the patterns behind declined and at-risk renewals. **Then:** This is its own recurring procedure, not a step here. Write it as [your monthly renewal review procedure]: on [day of month], [role] reads the declined and at-risk outcomes logged in step 7 and looks for patterns across accounts.

## Quality checklist

- [ ] Renewal record opened, end date and terms checked against the signed agreement, and an owner named
- [ ] Account health reviewed (support, usage, payment) before outreach, and any open issue resolved or planned
- [ ] Value recap built from actual outcomes delivered in the current term
- [ ] Proposal terms checked and initialed in the CRM, then sent on the proposal day with value presented before terms
- [ ] Follow-ups sent on the calendar: email, then call, then manager escalation
- [ ] Any price pushback met with a scope conversation before a discount, and any discount approved before it was offered
- [ ] Countersigned copy saved and CRM end date updated the same day
- [ ] Billing confirmation of new terms attached to the CRM record
- [ ] Outcome (renewed, declined, or no response, with reason) logged in the CRM

## Common mistakes

- **Mistake:** Waiting until the last month to start the renewal conversation. **Fix:** Run the monthly renewal report on schedule and treat each contract that appears on it as a standing task, not a reaction to an expiration alert.
- **Mistake:** Opening the renewal conversation with price and terms instead of value delivered. **Fix:** Build the one-page value recap first and lead the proposal with it, every time.
- **Mistake:** Discounting automatically the first time a client pushes back on price. **Fix:** Respond with a scope conversation first; only discount with a documented reason and approval.
- **Mistake:** Letting a signed renewal live only in the account lead's inbox. **Fix:** Update the CRM contract end date and send the new terms to billing the same day the agreement is signed.
- **Mistake:** Counting follow-up days from the proposal date instead of the contract end date. **Fix:** Every day count in this SOP is days before the contract end date. Write the actual dates in the CRM when you send the proposal.

## How to know it is working

- Every contract ending in the report window appears on the monthly report with an assigned owner.
- The client receives the value recap before or alongside the renewal terms, not after.
- No renewal lapses without a documented attempt to reach the client on the email, call, and escalation days.
- Price concessions, when made, are tied to a documented scope change or an approved exception, not a reflex.
- Every renewal outcome, win or decline, is logged in the CRM with the client's stated reason.
- Every renewed contract has a countersigned copy saved and a billing confirmation attached.

## Make it your procedure

1. Have the person who does this work fill in the [bracketed] parts and correct the steps. They know the details an owner skips.
2. Hand it to someone who has never done the task and watch them follow it without help. Every question they ask is a missing step. Add it.
3. When the work changes, change the procedure first, update the date under the title, and tell the people who use it what changed. Then nobody has a reason to work around it.
4. Save it with the others in one shared procedures folder, and fill in the procedure record at the bottom.
