# Performance Review

Updated September 28, 2026

The manager runs this procedure once for each employee or contractor at every formal review, on [your review cadence, e.g. quarterly or annually]. HR opens the cycle in step 1 and files the review in step 7. It ends when the signed review is filed and the next check-in is calendared: send the self-assessment, read it against the feedback log, write the rubric-based assessment, hold the two-way review meeting, document and acknowledge, and file. Goals and the running feedback log are set and kept before the cycle opens, so this procedure assumes they exist. It gives every manager one fair, evidence-based way to run a review without reinventing it each cycle.

**What this gives you:** Reviews that are fair, specific, and two-way let you help people become better at their work than they thought they could be. That is the part of leading a team that makes it worth building one.

**Primary owner:** Manager (direct supervisor)  
**Runs:** Once per employee at every formal review, on [your review cadence, e.g. quarterly or annually]  
**Time:** [2 to 3] hours of preparation plus a [45 to 60] minute review meeting per employee (example figures, replace with your own after your first cycle)

## Before you start

- A documented rubric with core competencies, a [rating scale, e.g. 1 to 5], and a behavioral anchor (a plain description of what that rating looks like in practice) for each rating level. HR keeps it current on [rubric review cadence], as its own routine outside this procedure.
- Goals or outcomes agreed at the start of the review period, in writing, tied to the role's real responsibilities (for example, [number of goals, e.g. 3 to 5] specific goals), and stored in [your goal tracker]
- A running feedback log kept by the manager through the whole review period, with both positive and constructive entries, updated at each check-in. It is a standing habit, not a step in this procedure.
- A self-assessment form and a manager assessment form
- A private meeting space, in person or video, with enough time blocked and not squeezed between other meetings
- A record-keeping location, [your HR system or employee file], to store the completed review

## Procedure

1. **Open the review cycle and send the manager the review packet** (Owner: HR)

   - **a.** Confirm the rubric version to use and write its version or date on the cycle record.
   - **b.** Send the manager the rubric, the self-assessment form, the manager assessment form, the employee's goals for the period, and the dates for this cycle: self-assessment due, manager assessment due, and review meeting.
   - **c.** Confirm the manager has the feedback log for the employee. If there are no entries for the period, tell the manager to proceed with whatever evidence exists and go to step 2.
   - **d.** If no goals were recorded for the period, tell the manager to use the responsibilities in [job description location], note "no goals on record" on the review record, and go to step 2.

   > **Use this wording: Cycle-open message to the manager**
   > 
   > Hi [manager name], the [period] review cycle is open for [employee name]. Attached: the rubric ([version or date]), both forms, and their goals. Dates: self-assessment out by [date], your assessment done by [date], review meeting by [date]. Tell me if anything is missing.

   *Why this matters:* A review is only as fair as the standard it is measured against. Handing the manager the same rubric, forms, and dates every cycle keeps reviews consistent across managers.

2. **Send the self-assessment** (Owner: Manager)

   - **a.** Send the self-assessment form to the employee [N] business days before the review meeting (e.g. 10), so there is real time to reflect and not a same-day request.
   - **b.** Book the review meeting in a private space (in person or video) with enough time blocked, and put its date and time in the same message.
   - **c.** Use the same structured questions every cycle: accomplishments against the agreed goals, challenges faced, and what support would help going forward.
   - **d.** If the form has not come back [N] business days before the meeting, send one reminder. If it is still not back the day before the meeting, note "self-assessment not returned" on the review record, give the employee the form to complete at the meeting, and go to step 3.

   > **Use this wording: Self-assessment request**
   > 
   > Hi [employee name], your [period] review meeting is on [date and time]. Please complete the attached self-assessment by [date]. It asks about what you achieved against your goals, what got in the way, and what support would help. I will read it before I write my assessment, so take the time you need.

   > **Use this wording: Reminder if not returned**
   > 
   > Hi [employee name], a reminder that your self-assessment for [period] is due [date]. If something is in the way, reply and let me know.

   *Why this matters:* A self-assessment gives the employee a voice in the review and shows the manager context or wins they did not see day to day.

3. **Read the self-assessment against the feedback log** (Owner: Manager)

   - **a.** Read the returned self-assessment in full before you write any rating. If it was not returned, skip this action and the action that lists where the self-assessment and the log disagree.
   - **b.** Read the feedback log for the whole period, oldest entry first, so the last few weeks do not outweigh the rest.
   - **c.** List each place the self-assessment and the log disagree, and each win in the self-assessment that is not in the log, so you can discuss them in the meeting.
   - **d.** If the log has no entries for part of the period, note the gap on the review record and go to step 4 with the evidence you have.

   *Why this matters:* Recency bias, judging the whole period by the last few weeks, is an easy review error to fall into. Reading the log first puts the whole period in front of you before you write a rating.

4. **Write the manager assessment from the rubric and the log** (Owner: Manager)

   - **a.** Score each competency against the documented rubric using the [rating scale], with the feedback log and goal outcomes as evidence, not general impressions.
   - **b.** Write specific, evidence-based comments for both strengths and growth areas: what happened, its impact, and the pattern it reflects.
   - **c.** If a goal was missed because of factors outside the employee's control, write the context next to that goal and rate what the person controlled.
   - **d.** Draft [number of goals, e.g. 3 to 5] goals for the next period, so the meeting ends with a forward plan, not just a look backward.

   *Why this matters:* Evidence-based, rubric-anchored assessments make reviews easier to defend if a rating is ever challenged.

5. **Run the review meeting as a two-way conversation** (Owner: Manager)

   - **a.** If the employee completed the self-assessment at the meeting, read it with them first and note on the review record anything that changes a rating.
   - **b.** Open with the opening line below, then go through specific strengths and wins from the period, each grounded in evidence.
   - **c.** Walk through growth areas the same way (behavior, impact, and the change expected) and invite the employee's perspective before moving on.
   - **d.** Agree on goals for the next period together, and note any support or resources the employee needs from you.
   - **e.** If the employee disagrees with a rating, go through the evidence behind it against the rubric.
   - **f.** If new evidence changes the picture, change the rating and write down why. If it does not, keep the rating and record the employee's disagreement in their own words on the review record. Then continue to step 6.
   - **g.** If the conversation surfaces a larger performance or conduct concern, tell HR the same day and start [your formal improvement or conduct procedure] as a separate documented track. Finish this review, then go to step 6.

   > **Use this wording: Opening line for the meeting**
   > 
   > Thanks for making time, [employee name]. I want this to be a conversation, not a readout. I will start with what went well this period, then the areas to grow, and I want your view at each point. Then we will agree on goals for [next period]. Before I start, is there anything you want to make sure we cover?

   > **Use this wording: Introducing a growth area**
   > 
   > One area I want to talk about is [competency]. What I saw was [specific behavior and date]. The impact was [impact]. What I would like to see is [expected change]. How does that match your view?

   *Why this matters:* A review the employee only listens to, rather than participates in, tends to produce compliance more than improvement or buy-in.

6. **Document the review and get acknowledgment** (Owner: Manager)

   - **a.** Write the final ratings, comments, and agreed next-period goals on the review record within [N] hours after the meeting (e.g. 24).
   - **b.** Give the employee the record and ask them to acknowledge it by signature or written confirmation, and add their comments if they wish. Say that acknowledgment means they received it, not that they agree with it.
   - **c.** If the employee declines to sign or acknowledge, write "Employee declined to acknowledge on [date]" on the record, initial it, and have HR countersign that the record was given to them. Then go to step 7.

   > **Use this wording: Asking for acknowledgment**
   > 
   > Please sign here to show you received this review. Signing does not mean you agree with every rating. You can add your own comments on the form, and they will be filed with it.

   *Why this matters:* A review that is not documented did not really happen. The acknowledgment, or the recorded refusal, makes the record usable if a rating is later questioned.

7. **File the review and calendar the next check-in** (Owner: HR)

   - **a.** File the signed or countersigned review in [record location] and write the filing date on the cycle record.
   - **b.** Confirm the file holds the review record, the self-assessment (or the "not returned" note), and the acknowledgment or the recorded refusal. If any is missing, get it from the manager before you close the cycle.
   - **c.** Calendar the next check-in for [N] weeks from now, so agreed goals are revisited before the next formal review and not only at it.
   - **d.** Send the manager and the employee the calendar invitation and close the cycle record.

   *Why this matters:* Goals agreed verbally and never revisited can teach employees the process does not matter, and a review that is not filed cannot be referenced later.

## Exceptions and troubleshooting

- **If** The employee strongly disagrees with a rating. **Then:** Follow step 5: walk through the specific evidence against the rubric. Change the rating only if new evidence changes the picture, and record the disagreement in the employee's own words if it does not.
- **If** The feedback log is thin because informal check-ins were not happening. **Then:** Note the gap on the review record, focus the review on what evidence does exist, and set a monthly check-in cadence with a running log entry after each one for the next period so this does not repeat. Goals and the log are kept outside this procedure, so fix them at the source.
- **If** A goal from the last period was missed because of factors outside the employee's control. **Then:** Document the context next to the goal and rate what the person could control. Reviews should not penalize people for shifting business conditions.
- **If** The review conversation surfaces a bigger performance or conduct concern. **Then:** Tell HR the same day and start [your formal improvement or conduct procedure] as a separate, documented track rather than folding it into the regular review.
- **If** Goals were never agreed at the start of the period, or the rubric is out of date. **Then:** Note it on the cycle record and use the role's written responsibilities for this cycle. Goal setting is its own routine at the start of each period, and rubric upkeep is HR's own routine on [rubric review cadence]. Neither runs inside this procedure, so schedule them separately.

## Quality checklist

- [ ] Cycle opened by HR with the rubric version, forms, goals, and cycle dates sent to the manager
- [ ] Self-assessment sent [N] business days before the meeting, and read before the manager assessment was written
- [ ] Feedback log read for the whole period, not just recent weeks
- [ ] Manager assessment scored against the rubric and the [rating scale] with specific, evidence-based comments
- [ ] Review meeting held as a two-way conversation, not a one-way readout
- [ ] Any disagreement with a rating recorded in the employee's own words
- [ ] Review record written within [N] hours of the meeting, with next-period goals agreed before the meeting ended
- [ ] Signed, or countersigned by HR if the employee declined, and filed in the employee record with the next check-in calendared

## Common mistakes

- **Mistake:** Basing the review mostly on the last 30 days instead of the full period. **Fix:** Read the whole feedback log, oldest entry first, in step 3 and write the assessment from it, not from memory at review time.
- **Mistake:** Writing vague comments like "good attitude" or "needs improvement" with no specific example. **Fix:** Anchor every comment to a specific behavior and its impact, tied to the rubric.
- **Mistake:** Turning the meeting into a monologue instead of a conversation. **Fix:** Send the self-assessment ahead of time and open the meeting with the opening line in step 5, which asks for the employee's perspective before you deliver your own.
- **Mistake:** Agreeing on next-period goals verbally and never writing them down or revisiting them. **Fix:** Document goals in the review record within [N] hours and calendar a check-in before the next formal review.
- **Mistake:** Treating a refusal to sign as the end of the review. **Fix:** Record the refusal, have HR countersign that the record was given to the employee, and file it anyway.

## How to know it is working

- The employee can describe their rating and the evidence behind it in their own words.
- Goals for the next period are specific, written down, and agreed by both people.
- Nothing in the review meeting was a surprise, because it had already surfaced in an earlier check-in.
- The review is signed, or the refusal is recorded and countersigned, and it is filed and referenced again at the next check-in.

## Make it your procedure

1. Have the person who does this work fill in the [bracketed] parts and correct the steps. They know the details an owner skips.
2. Hand it to someone who has never done the task and watch them follow it without help. Every question they ask is a missing step. Add it.
3. When the work changes, change the procedure first, update the date under the title, and tell the people who use it what changed. Then nobody has a reason to work around it.
4. Save it with the others in one shared procedures folder, and fill in the procedure record at the bottom.
